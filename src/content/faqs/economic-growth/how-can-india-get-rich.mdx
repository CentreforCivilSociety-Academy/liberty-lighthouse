question: How can India get rich?

Ask any economist, any minister, or any policy commentator what India needs to do to get rich, and they will give you a sector. Manufacturing, they will say. Or software services. Or semiconductors. Or green energy. The specific answer changes with the headline of the week. The structure of the argument does not. And that is the problem.

Sector prescriptions confuse the what with the how. They tell us what India should produce without asking what would make India productive. And the answer to that second question is different in kind — it is not about any single industry. It is about the conditions under which industries emerge.

Take manufacturing. The case for it is real. Manufacturing creates formal jobs at scale, drives exports, builds supply chains, and imposes a competitive discipline that is hard to replicate in more sheltered industries. There is a reason every country that got rich in the last century went through a manufacturing phase. But the reason manufacturing matters is not that factories are uniquely valuable. It is that the conditions manufacturing requires — property rights that hold, contracts that can be enforced, labour regulations that allow firms to hire and scale, land that can be acquired in reasonable time, infrastructure that reaches industrial clusters — are simply good conditions to have, full stop. Fix the conditions for manufacturing and you have fixed the conditions for services, for agriculture, for every sector where someone has an idea and wants to build something.

The same logic runs in reverse. A regulatory environment that makes it hard to build a factory makes it equally hard to scale a software firm, grow a restaurant chain, or start a fintech. The friction is not sectoral. It is structural. Which means that chasing sectors — picking manufacturing over services, or services over manufacturing — is the wrong debate. It reproduces, in a different form, the same mistake the Planning Commission made: the belief that someone in Delhi can identify the right answer before the market has had a chance to find it.

The actual answer is simpler and harder. A young person from a small town who wants to build something will find her own answer to what that something is. What she needs from the state is not a production-linked incentive scheme pointing her toward the approved sector. She needs a legal system that enforces her contracts, a regulatory environment in which she can hire and grow without ruinous overhead, and a market open enough that the best product wins. Get those conditions right, and the sectors take care of themselves.


Economic growth is not a plan. It is not a scheme. It is not a sector. It is what happens when millions of people — each pursuing their own idea of a better life — are free to try. India’s job is not to choose the answer. It is to get out of the way of the people who will find it.
