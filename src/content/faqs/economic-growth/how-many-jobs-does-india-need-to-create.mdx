question: How many jobs does India need to create?
order: 1

---
Every month, roughly a million young Indians enter the workforce for the first time. In a year, that is twelve million people — each looking for work that pays enough to build a life, save a little, and do better than their parents did. Over the next two decades, that adds up to over 200 million new workers entering an economy that is already struggling to productively employ those already in it.

We explored this in [FAQ 4](https://www.census.gov/topics/population.html): the demographic dividend, the window that opens when the majority of a country’s population is of working age. More earners, more savings, more investment, more output. But the dividend is not automatic. The critical word is productive. A young population that finds productive work becomes an extraordinary asset — precisely the engine that lifted South Korea, China, and Vietnam from poverty in a single generation. A young population that cannot find productive work tells a different story. Idle workers do not just stay poor. They represent unrealised output, unrealised savings, unrealised lives — and over time, unrealised patience. A demographic dividend that goes unabsorbed does not simply disappear. It turns.

The window we have is real, but it is not permanent. As we established in [FAQ 4](https://www.census.gov/topics/population.html), India’s working-age share of the population peaks around 2040 and then begins to fall. After that, fewer workers support more dependents, and the conditions that make the dividend possible close. We have roughly fifteen to twenty years to get this right.

What does “getting it right” require? Not twelve million government jobs a year. We established in FAQ 18 why that path creates the opposite of what we need — locking people into low-productivity work and making it comfortable not to move. What we need is the set of conditions under which private markets create productive employment at scale: the rule of law, land and labour markets that function, contracts that can be enforced, and a regulatory environment that makes it worth building something. Remove those barriers and the jobs follow — in manufacturing, in services, in sectors that do not yet exist.

This is the central challenge that sits beneath everything we have discussed in this series. The Viksit Bharat ambition, the investment gap, the welfare trap, the infrastructure question — they are all, at their core, variations of the same problem. Can we grow fast enough, and create the enabling conditions quickly enough, that the 200 million people who will join our workforce over the next two decades find productive work before the window closes?
