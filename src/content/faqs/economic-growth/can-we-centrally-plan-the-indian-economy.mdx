In 1950, [India's first Planning Commission](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Planning-Commission) sat down in Delhi to design the country's future. The idea of central planning was rooted in the country’s lived experience. India had just emerged from two centuries of British rule, in which private capital — first in the form of the East India Company, then British industry — had extracted wealth rather than built it. So the question was what private capital would do if left to itself. Indian industry in 1950 was thin and fragile. Domestic savings were low. Capital markets barely existed. It was plausible to think that private investors would not, on their own, build steel plants, dam rivers, or lay power grids, investments that leaders at the time felt an independent country urgently needed. Further, Soviet Union appeared to have shown that central planning worked: it had industrialized an agrarian economy in two decades and won the Second World War. In 1950, before the full picture of Soviet failure was visible, [Five-Year Plans](https://www.niti.gov.in/planning-commission-archive) looked like compelling evidence. 

So, the Planning Commission set targets — how much steel India needed, how much coal, how much grain, where factories should be built etc. Someone had to make these decisions and the government decided to play this role. But the problem was not the intention. It was the information.

Consider what it would take to plan the supply of food across India. The [Food Corporation of India](https://dfpd.gov.in/food-corporation-of-india/en) — which buys, stores, and distributes grain across the country — needs to know how much wheat farmers in Punjab will grow this season, whether the monsoon will hold, what consumers in Bengal are willing to pay, how much to move by rail and when, and how much to hold in reserve. They need to perfectly aggregate knowledge that is localized with the farmer, the trader, the miller and the consumer. And given that each of these factors changes dynamically. No committee in Delhi, however expert, can know all of it at once.

In a market, this knowledge and coordination problem is solved without anyone solving it. When wheat starts going out of stock, its price rises. That rising price tells farmers to plant more, tells consumers to use less, and tells traders to move stock from surplus regions to deficit ones, without a single instruction being issued. The price itself carries information that no central planner can gather or replicate. For a deeper explanation of this, see Hayek’s essay [The Use of Knowledge in Society](https://www.cato.org/sites/cato.org/files/articles/hayek-use-knowledge-society.pdf). And the moment planners fix prices, this signal disappears.

But even if a planner could somehow know what to produce, there is another problem that remains: why would anyone produce their products well, if everyone sells the produce at the same price? In a market, a business that produces lower quality goods, loses money and eventually closes. These are harsh outcomes, but they are also signals. They tell a producer when resources are being wasted and force those resources to move somewhere more useful. Without market signals – both domestic and global, producers remain inefficient.

Over time the Planning Commission had to come to face with this reality. Targets were consistently missed. Industries built behind protective walls never became competitive. And the economy that was supposed to be engineered into prosperity stagnated. All of this changed in 1991, when we started embracing the market and its signals.
